Scroll to explore

The first Indian woman to win a medal in a FIS-scored cross-country race.

Two bronzes in Chile in 2025, a World Cup start list at Ruka in the December that followed, and a sprint quarter-final at the Asian Winter Games. She reached all of it from a country with no cross-country course, no domestic race calendar and no coach in her discipline.

Who she is

It started with a film, and a race on television.

She grew up on her family's coffee farm at Perur, near Napoklu in Kodagu, where it has never snowed and where neither of her parents had ever seen snow either. In 2013 she watched a Bollywood film with a trekking sequence in it and went looking for the mountains it was set in. That took her to Darjeeling, to a mountaineering qualification, and to a job instructing in Kashmir, which is where she first put on skis.

Cross-country came later, and secondhand. Watching the 2018 Pyeongchang Games she saw the Norwegian Marit Bjørgen race, and decided that was the event she wanted. She left the instructing job to train full time. Her father, Thekkada Nanjunda, who has spent his life on the coffee farm, told her that if it made her happy she should do it.

The sport

The sport splits two ways, by technique and by distance. She races every combination.

  • 01Classic

    Skis run inside two grooves cut into the snow. Her sprint at Harbin was a classical race.

  • 02Freestyle

    A skating stride on a wide groomed piste, no grooves. Her 5 km at Harbin was freestyle, in 18:10.

  • 03Sprint

    About 1.3 to 1.5 km, run as knockout heats. She reached the quarter-final at Harbin in 2025.

  • 04Distance

    10 or 15 km, alone against the clock. Bronze at both distances at Gulmarg in 2026.

India has no permanent snow in her discipline, so her year follows it across both hemispheres: Kashmir and Europe from December to March, conditioning on the coast through the Indian summer, then South America or New Zealand in August and September.

01

Kodagu to Davos

It begins in a district that has never had snow and ends on a World Cup start list at Davos.

1995

Kodagu

Born 14 May 1995 at Perur, near Napoklu, in the coffee-growing hills of Kodagu. It rains hard there for four months a year and it has never snowed. The nearest snow sits two thousand kilometres north and four thousand metres up.

2014

The mountains came first

She started mountaineering at nineteen, trained at ABVIMAS and at the Himalayan Mountaineering Institute in Darjeeling, and qualified as a mountaineer and instructor by 2017. There was no ski club to come up through. The mountains did that job.

2016

The Republic Day contingent

Selected for the NCC contingent at the Republic Day Parade in New Delhi, a year after the All India NCC camp. Her way to altitude came through a cadet uniform.

2018

Snow, and then skis

She had seen snow for the first time only three years before. Skis arrived as equipment for the instructing job, and the same year she climbed Mount Elbrus, at 5,642 m the highest summit in Europe.

2019–2020

Certified abroad, then cross-country

She took an international ski instructor certification in New Zealand, on an active volcano, then spent a season skiing abroad. Cross-country followed in 2020, the endurance end of the sport she was already built for.

2022–

Onto the FIS circuit

Her first FIS-scored races were at Akureyri in northern Iceland, in January 2022. Planica followed in 2023 and Kazakhstan in 2024, then Harbin, Trondheim, Corralco and a first World Cup start at Ruka, all inside 2025.

Beyond the finish line

Qualifying is the target. It is not the whole of the ambition.

  • A sport with no home crowd

    Every season she competes in is assembled from other countries' calendars. There is no home fixture for anyone to turn up to.

  • Explaining the sport

    Most people in India have never watched a cross-country race. Part of the job is explaining what it is and why the distances are hard.

  • Athletes behind her

    A pathway that exists on paper is different from one somebody has already walked. Being the first is the easy part to celebrate. Being followed is the harder one, and it is the one she is working on.

  • Proving the ceiling is higher

    An Indian woman on a World Cup start list changes what the next selection meeting thinks is realistic.

08

Partnerships and support

She races a full FIS calendar largely at her own expense, and trains with other nations' squads because India fields none in her discipline. A season has a fixed shape, and support lands at particular points inside it.

Current support

  • Athlete support

    Athlete support through their sports programme: sports psychologist, nutritionist and strength coach, and conditioning blocks in Mumbai through the Indian summer.

  • State support

    State backing from her home state, the department that has recognised her results and carried her through domestic selection and the Khelo India programme.

What support funds

01

Training blocks on snow

The European season opens in late November against athletes who have been on snow since October. A pre-season block in Scandinavia or the Alps buys those six weeks back, and it is the difference between racing into form and racing to find it.

02

Competition travel

December 2025 ran Ruka to Trondheim to Davos inside sixteen days. The federation covers entry and accommodation on some of it. Flights, transfers and everything between the start lists are hers.

03

Coaching and wax service

Every other athlete on a World Cup start list has a service team reading the snow that morning and grinding skis to it. She does not. Wax chosen for the wrong temperature costs more time than any amount of fitness gets back.

04

Race skis and kit

Skis are ground for the snow they will meet, so one pair cannot cover a season. A full race set is classic and freestyle in cold, normal and warm grinds, with poles, boots and a race suit behind them.
